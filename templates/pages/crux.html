crux

An astrophotography processor, from raw subs to a finished image. In development

crux grades subs, finds the right calibration frames anywhere in your library, stacks, builds mosaics, and finishes the image, without moving or changing your raw data.

01

Sorts good subs from bad

crux measures every sub for focus, tracking, cloud, sky brightness and satellite trails, compares it with the rest of its night and its panel, and gives each frame a grade with a reason: “transparency 0.03: stars at 3% of their brightness in the field’s best frame”. Drag a reject limit and the whole night regrades as you drag, or override individual frames by hand.

02

Never touches your data

crux reads your files where they are. Its index and cache live in its own folder, and everything it makes (calibrated and registered frames, masters, mosaics and images) goes to a working folder that you can delete whenever you like.

If you want your folders tidied, crux can do that too: combine a target shot over several folders, or file subs by night, panel or filter. You see every move before it happens, and any change can be undone. Rejects are moved to a BAD folder, never deleted.

03

Finds the best flats, darks and bias in your whole library

Point crux at your top-level folders and it builds a calibration library: every flat set, dark and bias, checked and graded. When you process a target, crux picks the best calibration from anywhere in that library. No more copying flats, darks and bias into every target folder.

Flats are matched by night, filter and the dust pattern they share with your lights, and checked against the lights. Darks are matched by their hot pixels: a master dark from September covered 100% of the LMC lights’ hot pixels, while one from January covered only 26%.

If every flat set for a night fails its checks, crux still uses the one that does better than no flat at all, and tells you. Choose a set yourself and crux uses it, even one it rejected.

04

Recipes make the next night easy

Everything you set in the image stages (gradient removal, palette, stretch, finishing, star colours, sharpening and noise reduction, crop and turn) is kept as the target’s recipe. Add another night’s subs and crux processes only the new frames, stacks them in, and rebuilds the image with the same recipe. Save a recipe by name to start other targets from it.

05

Mosaics are automatic

Shoot the panels and crux does the rest. It plate-solves each panel’s master, fits the lens distortion, places the panels from where they are on the sky (not from their names), matches their brightness, and blends the seams.

This 36-panel LMC mosaic came together in 12 seconds: 176 megapixels, with stars in the overlaps lining up to 0.11 pixels.

06

Sharpen, denoise and remove stars your way

If you have an RC Astro licence, crux runs BlurXTerminator and NoiseXTerminator in its Image stage (and StarXTerminator from the command line) on your linear masters.

Or use crux’s own three neural networks, trained specifically for crux on real amateur data, including subs from ASV members, from four cameras: a noise reducer, a star corrector that rounds out elongated or comatic stars and can tighten them, and a star remover. New recipes correct star shapes and reduce noise with them by default.

On cameras it had never seen in training, the noise reducer gave faint areas the noise level of nearly seven times as many frames, while keeping star brightness within 1% (median). The star corrector took M 17’s stars from 0.55 to 0.38 eccentricity, keeping their flux within 1.5%.

With natural star colours, crux stretches the starless image and the stars separately and screens the stars back on, so you can make stars smaller without touching the nebula. Round glows that star removal leaves around bright stars go back with the stars, so narrowband halos don’t come out magenta.

07

Mono or one-shot colour

crux works with mono cameras and filters (narrowband SHO and HOO with the palette mixer’s nine presets, LRGB, LRGB with H-alpha) and with one-shot colour cameras, including dual-band filters. It has processed data from the ASI2600MM, an SBIG STL-11000M and a QHY9 from 2010–2012, and one-shot colour from an ASI294MC Pro and a Seestar S30. One-shot colour support is the newest part of crux.

08

Easy to use, with visual feedback

Every stage shows a before and after split that updates as you move a control: most previews redraw in under 20 milliseconds and natural star colours in under 70, and regrading a night takes about 10. Each step says in plain words what it does and whether it has run. Settings show whether they’re the default, from your recipe, or changed here, and a click on “changed here” puts a setting back.

09

Fast and native

crux is written in Rust: every step is native code, using all your CPU cores. Calibrating and registering a 26-megapixel sub takes about a second, and measuring 302 subs takes 47 seconds.

The networks, and the warps that line up frames and mosaic panels, run on your graphics card: NVIDIA, AMD, Intel or Apple, through DirectX 12, Vulkan or Metal, with no vendor libraries to install. On an RTX 3070 the networks run about 15 times faster than on an 8-core CPU, and the whole 176-megapixel LMC image with noise reduction, star correction and star removal takes about a minute instead of nearly ten. Without a suitable card, crux uses the CPU and gets the same result. The top bar shows which one it’s using.

crux runs on Windows. Builds for Linux and macOS (Apple Silicon) are made the same way, but haven’t been tested on a real machine yet.

Status

crux is in development. It’s being built and tested on real data from N.I.N.A., PixInsight’s WBPP, MaxIm DL and the ASIAIR.

crux is open source under the MIT licence. The code is on GitHub, and downloads for Windows, Linux and macOS are on its Releases page.
